# Chart Chooser
The FSO Chart Chooser is an interactive tool that helps you pick the right chart type for your data and for what you want to say. You can find it at <a href="https://www.chartchooser.bfs.admin.ch/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">chartchooser.bfs.admin.ch</a>
 
The right chart makes your data easy to read. The wrong chart makes readers work harder, or can even make them misunderstand the data.
The Chart Chooser helps you decide by asking three simple questions:
*What is the main message you want to show?*, *How many variables do you want to show?* and *What type of variables are they?*

## How the tool works
 
The Chart Chooser uses two steps to help you go from your message to the right chart type:
 
**Step 1 : What relationship do you want to show?**

**Step 2 : What is your data structure?** 

Narrow down your options by the number of variables (one variable / several variables) and their type (numbers / categories, discrete / continuous).
 
The tool then shows you only the chart types that work for your choices. Each result links to a template page with: who the chart is for, what questions it answers, what it needs (how many dimensions), and examples that follow FSO standards.

## Why this matters for readability
 
The chart type you pick affects every other readability choice, because it decides how your data is shown visually. If you pick the wrong chart type, no amount of fine-tuning later will fix it.
 
It's easy to skip this step when you're in a hurry, but the Chart Chooser reminds you to do it anyway: **start from your message, not from a chart type you're used to.** A column chart or a pie chart can feel like a safe, easy choice. But if your story is about how parts make up a whole, a stacked or normalised chart may show the same data much more clearly.
 
FSO publications reach many kinds of readers, from policy experts to the general public, so this step matters even more. The tool brings together what FSO has learned about which chart types work well for which tasks. This means less guesswork for you, and more consistency across teams and publications.
 
## Recommended workflow
 
1. **Write down your message first.** Before you open the tool, write one sentence that says what you want the reader to understand. Keep it to a single message. This sentence will point you to the relationship category you need.
2. **Use the two-step filter.** Go through Step 1 (relationship) and Step 2 (data structure) instead of browsing the full list of charts. The filter exists to narrow things down to what actually works for you.
3. **Compare the charts you're left with.** When the tool gives you several valid chart types, read the template page for each one. Look closely at the *points of attention* section, it lists the limits and common mistakes for each type.
4. **Match the complexity to your audience.** The tool shows chart types of different complexity levels. Some, like a butterfly chart or a Marimekko, can be very precise but harder to read at a glance. Choose the simpler option when the extra precision doesn't really matter for your case.
5. **Use the template as a starting point.** The template pages give you example data and layouts that follow FSO standards. Check them before you build a new chart from scratch.

## Scope of the tool
 
The Chart Chooser covers many chart types, including standard statistical charts, combined charts (like Column and Line together), and thematic maps (Choropleth, Proportional Symbols, Flows). It does not cover tables or infographics, those have their own separate guidance.
 
For maps, keep in mind that choosing the right map involves extra choices (projection, classification, symbols) that go beyond what the Chart Chooser covers.
 

## Do / Don't
 
### Do
 
- Use the Chart Chooser whenever you're not sure which chart type to pick.
- Use the template pages as a checklist: they tell you the audience, the data structure, and the known limitations of each chart.
- Share the tool link with colleagues or clients who are defining what a chart should show, so everyone works from the same framework.
### Don't
 
- Don't pick a chart type just because it looks interesting or new. New and unusual charts are harder to read. Stick with familiar chart types unless there's a clear reason not to.
- Don't skip Step 2 (data structure). The relationship alone isn't enough: a line chart and a multi-line chart both show change over time, but they're not equally easy to read when you have several variables.
- Don't assume the tool's suggestions are the only options. If none of the suggested charts feel right, go back and check your Step 1 choice, you may need to rethink how you framed the relationship.
- Don't try to show several messages in one chart. One message = one chart.
