# Visual Hierarchy

Visual hierarchy means guiding the reader: what to look at first, what to look at second, and what to look at last. In a well-built chart or publication, every element has a place in a clear order, and its size, colour, position, and framing all support that order instead of working against it.

For FSO publications, which reach everyone from policy experts to the general public, visual hierarchy isn't just about looking nice. It's a practical need: if a chart has no clear hierarchy, the reader has to figure out the reading order themselves, which takes more effort and makes it more likely they'll misread the chart. Building in a clear hierarchy removes that extra work for the reader.

Visual hierarchy is built using five main tools: **position**, **size**, **colour**, **layering**, and **enclosure**. Each is explained below, along with FSO-specific rules where they apply.

## Position

The simplest way to build hierarchy is through placement. In left-to-right languages, including all FSO publication languages, readers start at the top left of a page or chart and finish at the bottom right. This reading pattern is consistent, so you can rely on it.

The order of importance for positions in a design is:

> top left > top right > bottom left > bottom right

Elements placed in each of these positions should be as important as their place in this order. In practice, for FSO charts:

- The **title** goes in the top-left. It's the first thing the reader sees, so it needs to be the most visible text on the chart.
- The **subtitle** or explanatory line goes right below the title, in a smaller and lighter style. It gives context or units that the title alone can't fit.
- **Annotations** that explain something in the data sit close to the data they're explaining, less prominent than the title and subtitle but more prominent than axis labels.
- **Axis labels and scale values** sit at the edges of the plot area. They're there for reference, without competing with the data itself.
- **Source lines, notes, and credits** go at the bottom of the chart. They're required for institutional standards, but they should be the least visible text on the page.

When you place several charts in a publication or dashboard, think about the order readers will see them in. Charts placed higher up and further left will be seen first. Make sure the order of your story matches this reading order, so readers see context before they see the conclusion.

Keep in mind that a chart can grab attention regardless of its position: a striking chart can pull the eye even if it comes later in the reading order. That's why FSO charts should be able to stand on their own, everything needed to understand a chart should be built into the chart itself, not left for the surrounding text to explain.

## Size

Bigger elements feel more important than smaller ones. If the size difference is big enough, it can override position entirely: a very large chart placed further down the page can catch the eye before a small one at the top left.

Within a single chart, the size of text elements should match their place in the reading order. The FSO order for chart text, from biggest to smallest, is:

> Title > Subtitle > Annotations > Axis labels, notes and value labels > Footer elements

Each level should be clearly smaller than the one above it, enough that anyone can see the difference right away, without needing to look closely. If a title and subtitle are the same size, the hierarchy disappears, and the reader has to work out what's important just from the words, instead of how they're shown.

In dashboards or layouts with several charts, you can give a key chart more visual weight by making it bigger than the others. Size shows priority. If a key chart is the same size as everything around it, it loses its status as the main focus.

## Colour

Colour is one of the most powerful hierarchy tools, because it works alongside size and position instead of competing with them. Even a small element in a low-priority position can stand out if it uses a strong or contrasting colour.

The FSO rule for using colour to show hierarchy inside a chart is:

- **Accent colour** (the FSO primary colour, or the strongest colour in the palette) for the data or category that carries the chart's main message. This is what the reader should notice first.
- **Black** for the most important text: the title, subtitle, and any annotations central to the message.
- **Light grey** for supporting elements: gridlines, tick marks, axis lines.

This means colour does two jobs at once: it shows which category something belongs to, and it shows how important it is. These two jobs shouldn't send different signals.

A common mistake is using a strong, saturated colour on things that aren't data : like logos, borders, or source lines. For example, a red logo in the bottom-right corner pulls the eye to the lowest-priority spot on the chart, which works against the hierarchy you're trying to build. Non-data elements should stay visually quiet, so they don't compete with the data.

## Layering

Layering is a more subtle hierarchy tool. It matters most for charts with overlapping data or annotations placed over a plot area.

Elements placed in front of (on top of) others feel more important. In practice:

- Annotation lines and labels should sit in front of gridlines and axis lines, never behind them.
- When data series overlap (like in area charts or stacked charts), put the series that carries the main message in front of the ones that are just there for context.
- Reference lines (like a European average or a regulatory threshold) should sit above the grid, but stay visually less important than the data. They are usually shown as a dashed or lighter line rather than a bold, solid one.

## Enclosure

Enclosure means putting a border or shaded area around an element to make it look grouped and separate from everything else. Enclosed elements draw attention.

Because of this, use enclosure carefully and sparingly. Good uses of enclosure in FSO charts include:

- A **light shaded background area** over a specific time period in a line chart (for example, a recession or a policy period), to show that this period is different from the rest.
- A **callout box** for an annotation that's too long to fit as a simple inline label.

Don't use enclosure just to decorate elements that aren't meant to stand out. A heavy border around a legend box, a framed source line, or a boxed subtitle all make those elements seem more important than they should be. As explained in the [Chart Junk guidelines](/docs/readability-best-practices-chart-junk--docs), borders and boxes on elements that don't need extra attention are a form of chart junk. In terms of hierarchy, they're actually misleading, because they suggest importance that isn't really there.

## Consistency across a publication

In publications, reports, and dashboards with several charts, visual hierarchy needs to stay the same across all of them. If the same type of element (titles, annotations, axis labels) looks different from one chart to the next (different sizes, colours, or weights), readers can no longer trust what they see as a signal of importance. They have to work out the hierarchy again for every single chart, which takes extra effort.

FSO's institutional standards already give you a typography and colour system. Used consistently, it sets up hierarchy automatically across all your outputs. If you need to deviate from it, write down why, and don't let that change spread to the rest of the publication.

## Do / Don't

### Do

- Put the title top-left, and make sure it's the biggest and most visible text on the chart.
- Follow the FSO size order: title > subtitle > annotations > data labels > axis labels > footer. Make the size differences clear, not just barely noticeable.
- Make sure the reading order in multi-chart layouts follows your story: place context charts before conclusion charts, in that spatial order.

### Don't

- Don't give a strong or saturated colour to non-data elements (logos, borders, source lines). It makes them seem more important than intended.
- Don't flatten the size scale: titles and subtitles at the same size, or axis labels and data labels at the same size, removes the signal that size is supposed to give.
- Don't put the most important chart in a dashboard somewhere the reading order reaches last. If a chart has to break the natural reading order, use size to make up for it.
- Don't use enclosure just for decoration. A border or box suggests something is important. If it isn't, the border is misleading.
- Don't let hierarchy change inconsistently between charts in the same publication. Once a visual property (size, colour, position) is used to show importance in one chart, it needs to mean the same thing in every chart that follows.
