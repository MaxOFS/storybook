# Chart Objectives

Every chart has two kinds of goals: what it's trying to say (its **communicative intent**) and what it needs to get right as a finished visualisation (its **production standards**). Both need to be defined before you build the chart. A chart that gets one right but not the other isn't ready to publish.

This page explains how to define and check both. The communicative intent section links directly to the [FSO Chart Chooser](/docs/readability-best-practices-chart-chooser--docs), which turns your intent into a chart type. The production standards section covers the baseline that applies to every FSO chart, no matter its type.

A good starting point is the difference between **exploratory** and **explanatory** data visualisation (data.europa.eu, 2023).

In **exploratory** visualisation, the chart is a tool for the analyst themselves. The goal is to get familiar with the data, spot patterns, and come up with ideas to test. Design quality isn't the priority here, speed and working smoothly with the data matter more. Exploratory charts aren't meant to be published.

In **explanatory** visualisation, the chart is meant to communicate something to someone else. The goal is to get a specific pattern or message across to a particular audience. Here, every design choice affects whether the message actually reaches the reader. All FSO publication charts are explanatory, and need to be designed that way from the start.

In practice, this means you can't just take a chart from exploratory work and publish it as-is. Even if an exploratory chart happened to reveal something interesting, it still needs a proper design pass before publication: defining the message, choosing the right chart type, applying visual hierarchy, and checking it meets all production standards.

## Communicative intent

Communicative intent is one sentence that says what you want your target audience to understand from the chart. You need to write this before making any design decisions, because everything else depends on it.

A good communicative intent statement has two parts:

1. **The data relationship**: what kind of pattern the chart shows (comparison, distribution, trend over time, part-to-whole, etc.)
2. **The finding**: what the data actually shows within that pattern (which category is highest, how the trend changed, which share is biggest)

### Connecting intent to chart type: the Chart Chooser

Your communicative intent statement points to the **data relationship** your chart needs to show. This relationship is what you feed into the [FSO Chart Chooser](https://www.chartchooser.bfs.admin.ch/en/), which matches relationship types to valid chart types.

If your intent doesn't fit one of these categories, it needs to be sharper. For example, a chart that tries to show two unrelated things at once will almost always fail to communicate either one clearly. The USWDS data visualisation guidance makes this point directly: keep the "big idea" of a visualisation to one central theme, with no more than two or three supporting ideas, to make it easier to take in (USWDS, 2022). It's usually better to limit how many messages you try to fit into one chart, even for expert audiences.

Once your intent is clear and you've identified the relationship category, use the Chart Chooser to get the shortlist of valid chart types for that relationship and data structure. Pick the simplest option on that list that gets your message across clearly for your audience, without losing precision.

## Production standards

Beyond communicative intent, every FSO chart needs to meet four institutional standards: **accuracy**, **clarity**, **reproducibility**, and **accessibility**. These aren't steps to do one after another, check all four before publishing.

### Accuracy

A chart is accurate when what it shows visually matches the real size of the values, and when the data behind it is correct and up to date.

Accuracy problems come in two forms. **Data errors**, like wrong values, outdated figures, or incorrect totals, happen before the chart is even built, and no amount of design work can fix them. Data needs to be checked before you start building the chart. **Visual distortions**, like a y-axis that doesn't start at zero, inconsistent scales, or 3D effects, happen while building the chart, and are fully within the designer's control. Both are covered in more detail in the [Chart Junk](/story/readability-best-practices-chart-junk--docs) section of the guidelines.

For FSO publications, all data needs to be traceable to a source, and the source line must appear on every chart. If the data has been processed in any way (aggregated, smoothed, indexed), write down how, either in the chart note or in the metadata.

### Clarity

A chart is clear when a reader with the expected level of data knowledge can understand its message without needing help. What counts as clear depends on the audience: a chart for policy experts can reasonably use more complex encodings than one aimed at the general public.

Clarity means:

- **One main message.** If a reader can't spot the main point within a few seconds of looking at the chart, it's not clear enough. You can include secondary findings, but they need to stay visually in the background.
- **Everything the reader needs, built in.** The chart must include a title, a unit, a scale, a source, and any notes needed to explain anything that isn't obvious. You can't rely on the surrounding text to supply this — publication charts need to stand on their own.
- **The right level of complexity for the audience.** USWDS guidance suggests sticking to common chart types when you don't know your audience's data literacy, and using familiar forms (bar charts, line charts) for explanatory charts aimed at the general public (USWDS, 2022).

### Reproducibility

A chart is reproducible when someone else could rebuild it using only what's written down about it. The whole path from raw data to finished chart should be easy to follow and easy to check — for anyone, not just the person who made it.

This means the data needs to be somewhere everyone can reach. It shouldn't sit only in someone's personal folder, an email, or a tool only one person can open. Link directly to the dataset (.STAT/STAT-TAB, a published table, an open data source) whenever you can, instead of using a private copy.
If the data was filtered, added up, indexed, or smoothed before going into the chart, write that down in the chart note or the metadata. **A reader should be able to go from the published table to the chart without having to guess a missing step.**
### Accessibility

A chart is accessible when its information reaches all readers, including people using assistive technology or people who can't reliably tell colours apart. See the [FSO Accessibility guidelines](/docs/accessibility-introduction--docs) for the full requirements.

## Aesthetics
A chart can be accurate, clear, reproducible, and accessible, and still fail to engage the reader if it looks flat or thrown together. Aesthetic quality isn't something you add on top of the other goals, and it doesn't compete with them either. It's really a mix of all our standards and rules together. It draws on standard visualisation principles, and should look the same way across all our published charts.

Readers are more likely to engage with, trust, and remember a chart that's well-made than one that's merely correct. If you treat aesthetic care as optional, or as something to do only if there's time left, you end up with charts that technically meet every standard above but still fail at the one thing that matters: getting the message read at all.

## Do / Don't

### Do

- Write your communicative intent as one sentence before opening any chart tool. Let that sentence drive every design choice that follows.
- Use the FSO Chart Chooser to go from intent to chart type. If the shortlist doesn't feel right, go back and rework your intent statement.
- Put the finding in the title. Save the subtitle for context (unit, geography, time period) that the title can't fit.
- Check all four production standards : accuracy, clarity, reproducibility, accessibility  before publishing. Treat them as a checklist to go through together, not one after another.
- Design for your audience's level of data knowledge, not your own. A chart that's clear to the analyst who built it isn't necessarily clear to the person reading it.

### Don't

- Don't start building a chart before you've defined the communicative intent. A chart built around whatever data you have, rather than around a message, will rarely communicate clearly.
- Don't try to show more than one main relationship in a single chart. If your data supports two separate findings, consider making two charts.
- Don't use a title that just names the dataset instead of the finding. A label isn't a message.
- Don't treat the data table as a full accessibility substitute for the chart. The text alternative needs to convey the trend or finding, not just the raw values.
- Don't publish a chart without a documented production trail. If the data changes or an error turns up, you need to be able to fix the chart.
