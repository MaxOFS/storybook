# Chart Junk

Chart junk is anything in a chart that doesn't help the reader understand the data. Edward Tufte first used the term in *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983). He described it as graphical elements that aren't needed, or that actively get in the way, decoration that competes with the data for the reader's attention.

Removing harmful chart junk is one of the most reliable ways to make a chart easier to read. Every pixel that doesn't carry information, and that misleads the reader, makes the chart harder to read for no benefit. This matters a lot for FSO publications, since they need to work across many formats and audiences: something that looks like harmless decoration on a big screen can become unreadable in print or on a small screen.

## Two categories of chart junk

Not all non-data visual elements do the same amount of harm. Robert Kosara's distinction between **harmful junk** and **harmless junk** is a useful way to think about this, and it matches what most people already do naturally, even if they don't use these exact words.

**Harmful junk** gets in the way of reading and understanding a chart. It distorts the values shown, hides data, or makes the chart harder to interpret correctly. This kind of chart junk should always be removed. Examples include 3D effects that distort the size of things, busy background images that reduce contrast with the data, and decorative shapes that hide where a bar or line actually ends.

**Harmless junk** is decoration that doesn't help readers understand the chart, but doesn't really hurt either. A study by Bateman et al. (2010) found that people remembered decorated charts better three weeks later, without losing their ability to understand the chart's message. This doesn't mean you should add decoration just for its own sake but it does mean the choice to include or leave out non-data elements should depend on the chart's specific purpose and audience.

FSO publications need readers to read exact values, need to be trusted, and need to look consistent across different outputs. For these reasons, FSO charts should lean towards the minimalist end of the scale. The goal isn't to strip the chart down as much as possible, it's to make sure every element in the chart earns its place.

## Graphical integrity and distortion

Chart junk is about too much decoration. A related but different problem is **graphical integrity**: making sure the size of what's shown in the chart matches the size of what it represents in the data. Tufte calls this the **Lie Factor**: the size of the effect shown in the chart, divided by the size of the effect in the real data. A Lie Factor of 1 means the chart is accurate. Values well above or below 1 mean the chart is distorted, whether on purpose or by accident.

The most common causes of this kind of distortion are:

- **Non-zero baselines.** If the y-axis doesn't start at zero, differences between bars look bigger than they really are. For example, a bar chart with a y-axis that starts at 90 instead of 0 can make a small difference look dramatic. For FSO bar and column charts, the baseline should always start at zero, unless there's a clear, documented reason not to.
- **Inconsistent scales.** Using uneven spacing on an axis, or different scales on charts that are meant to be compared side by side, changes how readers see the relationship between the values. Charts that are meant to be compared should always use the same scale.
- **Excessive pie slicing.** Pie charts with many small slices are hard to read, you can't tell the categories apart. As a rule, if a pie chart has more than five or six slices, use a ranked bar chart instead.
- **Pictograms as scaled encodings.** Replacing bars with icons that are sized to show a value causes area distortion: a coin that's twice as tall looks about four times bigger in area. Readers can't correct for this on their own, no matter how carefully they look.

Distortion is a different problem from decoration, but the two are related: both mean ink is being used in a way that misrepresents the data instead of showing it clearly.

## What counts as harmful chart junk

Here are the most common types of harmful chart junk you'll come across.

### Decorative fills and patterns

Replacing plain, solid colours with textures, gradients, or hatching patterns doesn't add any information. It breaks up the visual surface and makes it harder to compare areas. Tufte specifically criticised decorative hatching in bars and area charts, since it adds visual noise without helping readers understand the chart. For FSO charts, use flat colours from the institutional colour palette. Textures should only be used when colour alone can't tell elements apart.

### Unnecessary gridlines and tick marks

Gridlines are there to help readers estimate values. If gridlines are too dense, or darker than the data itself, they end up doing the opposite: pulling attention away from the data. In most cases, light horizontal gridlines at the main intervals are enough. Vertical gridlines are rarely needed in bar or line charts. Minor tick marks just add visual noise without helping readers read values more precisely.

### 3D effects

Rendering 2D data in 3D introduces distortion. The angle the reader views the chart from changes how big the values appear to be. For example, in a 3D pie chart, the slices at the front look bigger than equal-sized slices at the back. There is no case in FSO data visualisation where a 3D effect makes a chart more accurate or easier to understand. Don't use them.

### Redundant data labels

Showing both a y-axis scale and a label on every single bar repeats the same information twice. Pick one: either use a clear axis scale so readers can read approximate values, or add labels just for the values that matter most to your point. Labelling every single data point is only worth doing when exact values are essential for all categories.

### Busy background images and fills

Background images behind the chart reduce the contrast between the data and the background, making it harder to read values. This is a clear case of harmful junk: the background doesn't carry any information, and it actively makes the data harder to see. Chart backgrounds should be white or transparent.

### Heavy frames and borders

Heavy borders around the chart area, around legends, or around individual chart elements add visual weight without adding any real structure. A light border, or no border at all, usually looks cleaner. If a chart has a frame, it should stay in the background rather than compete with the content.

## The data-ink ratio

Tufte describes the idea behind removing chart junk with the concept of the **data-ink ratio**: the share of a chart's ink that's used to show actual data, rather than decoration or structure. A high data-ink ratio means most of what the reader sees is data. A low ratio means a lot of the chart is taken up by elements that don't carry any information.

In other words: `data-ink ratio = data-ink / total ink used to print the graphic`

The goal isn't to use as little ink as possible. Some non-data elements are necessary : axes, a legend, a title, source lines. The goal is to make sure every non-data element earns its place by helping readers understand the chart. In practice, ask this about each visual element: *if I removed this, would the reader lose information, or find the chart harder to understand?* If the answer is no to both, remove it or make it lighter.

## Do / Don't

### Do

- Remove or lighten gridlines until the data clearly stands out more than they do.
- Use flat, solid colours from the FSO colour palette. Avoid gradients, textures, and patterns, except where a chart needs to work in greyscale.
- For every visual element, ask: would removing this cost the reader any information or understanding? If not, remove it.
- Tell the difference between harmful junk (distorts or hides data) and harmless decoration (doesn't get in the way of reading).
- Start bar and column chart baselines at zero. If you make an exception, write down the reason clearly.
- Check your charts at the size they'll actually be shown (print, screen, small multiples). Chart junk and distortion are often easier to spot at smaller sizes.
- Use the same scale across all charts that are meant to be compared directly.

### Don't

- Don't use 3D effects on any chart. They distort the values shown, and readers can't correct for this on their own.
- Don't put images or coloured backgrounds behind the chart area. A white or transparent background gives the best contrast with the data.
- Don't put heavy borders around chart elements (legend boxes, plot areas). Let white space do that job instead.
- Don't let decoration take over the shape of the chart (the "Big Duck" problem). If the shape of the chart stands out more than the data it shows, something has gone wrong.
- Don't use pie charts with more than five or six slices. Use a ranked bar chart instead.
- Don't confuse simplicity with losing information. Removing chart junk should make the data easier to see, not harder.
